# Long-Run Fiscal & Monetary Regimes

**What this is:** the multi-century foundation for how heavily-indebted sovereigns actually exit war-scale debt, how the monetary regime itself silently changed under our feet in 1971, and why the size of the state only ever ratchets one way. This is the structural backdrop against which today's grow-out / inflate-out / default trilemma should be read. Built from GMD's centuries-deep fiscal and price history — the part of the record that FRED simply does not contain.

---

## 1. Two roads out of war debt

History offers exactly two clean, large-scale templates for working off war-scale sovereign debt, and they used opposite mechanisms.

| Episode | Start debt/GDP | End debt/GDP | Span | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **UK after Napoleon** | **223.8%** (1815) | **26.9%** (1914) | **99 years** | Real growth + primary surpluses under the **gold standard** |
| **US after WWII** | **121.5%** (1946) | **41.2%** (1981) | **35 years** | Largely the **inflation channel / financial repression** |

The contrast is the whole lesson:

- **The UK took the hard road and the slow road.** Under the gold standard there was no inflation lever to pull — and deflation actually *raised* the debt ratio along the way, working against the deleveraging. The UK had to out-grow and out-save the debt over the better part of a century, running primary surpluses decade after decade. Nominal discipline came at the cost of time: **99 years**.
- **The US took the soft road and the fast road.** Post-WWII, with the gold link loosening and a cooperative central bank, the US inflated and financially-repressed its way from **121.5%** to **41.2%** in just **35 years** — a deleveraging that the gold-standard UK could not have engineered.

Same problem (war-scale debt), opposite tools, a ~3x difference in the time to resolution. The inflation channel is faster; it is also a transfer from bondholders to the sovereign.

## 2. The 1971 inflation-regime break

The mechanism that made the US road possible is visible in the price record itself. The US inflation process is not one regime — it is two, with a hard break at the end of the gold-standard era.

| US inflation regime | Avg inflation | Years in deflation | Std dev |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1800–1970** (gold-standard norm) | **0.85%** | **37.4%** of years | 5.99 |
| **1971–present** (fiat) | **3.81%** | **1.7%** of years | 2.76 |

Read this carefully — it reframes the entire intuition most investors carry about inflation:

- Under the gold standard, **frequent deflation was normal**: prices fell in **37.4%** of all years, and average inflation was a near-flat **0.85%**. The price level was a round trip, not a one-way escalator. Volatility was high (std **5.99**) but mean-reverting.
- Since 1971, deflation has all but vanished — only **1.7%** of years — and the average has nearly quintupled to **3.81%**, with *lower* volatility (std **2.76**). The fiat regime delivers steadier but structurally positive inflation.

This is the single most important regime fact for a long-horizon book: **deflation was the historical baseline and inflation the deviation; post-1971 that polarity flipped.** Any model trained only on the post-WWII / post-1971 sample is fitting the anomaly, not the norm. It also explains Section 1 — the US could inflate out of WWII debt precisely because the regime had shifted to one where the price level reliably rises. (Cross-reference the US own-history percentile in [[00 - GMD Overview & Structural Edge]]: 2024 CPI of 2.9% still sits at the 67th percentile of the full 1721- history.)

## 3. The Leviathan ratchet — UK government spending

If inflation is the monetary regime story, the size of the state is the fiscal one. The UK's general government spending/GDP gives the cleanest multi-century read.

> **UK government spending/GDP: 6.9%** (late 1680s) → **44.6%** (2030, incl. IMF projection).

A **~6.5x** rise over roughly three and a half centuries. The path was not smooth — wars ratcheted it up, peace never fully ratcheted it back down — but the direction is monotonic across the long run. The state that taxed and spent **6.9%** of national income under the late Stuarts now commands **44.6%**.

For a macro PM this matters in two ways:

1. **The fiscal baseline only rises.** Structural deficits, entitlement growth, and crisis-response spending are the modern face of the same ratchet. Don't model a return to a small-state baseline that the historical record says never arrives.
2. **A bigger state changes the debt-dynamics arithmetic.** With government claiming ~45% of GDP, the primary-surplus path the UK used after Napoleon (Section 1) is far harder to replicate — there is less slack to squeeze, and the political cost of squeezing is higher. This tilts modern advanced economies toward the *inflation* exit rather than the *austerity* exit.

## 4. The trilemma for today: grow out / inflate out / default

The history above collapses into a single decision tree for any over-indebted sovereign. There are only three ways down from a high debt ratio:

| Road | Historical template | Requires | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grow out** | UK 1815→1914 | Sustained real growth **+** primary surpluses | Slow (99 yrs); needs a small-state, disciplined fiscal regime |
| **Inflate out** | US 1946→1981 | A fiat regime + financial repression | Faster (35 yrs); a stealth transfer from bondholders |
| **Default** | (the third road) | A willingness threshold | Median default hit at just **61%** debt/GDP — there is no safe level |

The framing for the current book:

- **Grow-out is structurally harder now.** The Leviathan ratchet (Section 3) leaves little fiscal slack, and post-1971 real growth has not consistently outrun the debt service that high ratios imply. GMD's base-rate work (the debt→forward-growth relationship) shows growth declining as debt rises, with a clear break above 60% — and the US (122%) and Japan (236%) sit in the top buckets today. See [[00 - GMD Overview & Structural Edge]].
- **Inflate-out is the path of least resistance** — and it is the one the post-1971 regime is built for. The 1946→1981 US precedent is the live template. This is the core reason a long-horizon macro book should treat structurally-positive inflation, not deflation, as the regime default.
- **Default is always on the menu, even at "low" debt.** The 61% median-at-default figure and the absence of any universal safe level mean the *willingness*-and-FX-denomination question (not just the level) governs the tail. Reserve-currency issuers with own-currency debt sit furthest from this road — which is exactly why they lean on the inflate-out lever instead.

## What it means for positioning

- **Treat post-1971 positive inflation as the regime baseline, not deflation.** The 0.85%/37%-deflation world is gone; the 3.81%/1.7%-deflation world is what a long book lives in. Structurally long real assets / short long-duration nominal debt is the regime-consistent default tilt for over-indebted reserve issuers.
- **Expect the inflate-out road, not the grow-out road, for the US and other large advanced economies.** The Leviathan ratchet and the 1971 break both point the same way; the UK's 99-year austerity grind is not the modern base case.
- **Don't anchor on a "safe" debt level.** 61% was the median at default; high-debt sovereigns with own-currency, deep-market debt have survived far higher. The signal is FX-denomination, rollover capacity, and willingness — not the headline ratio. Cross-reference the live regime read in [[Macro Regime - Live (June 2026)]] and the framework in [[The Global Macros Framework]].
- **Watch real long rates against this backdrop.** A high-debt sovereign financing itself at historically cheap real rates (the US tension flagged in [[00 - GMD Overview & Structural Edge]]) is exactly the financial-repression setup that powered the 1946→1981 deleveraging.
