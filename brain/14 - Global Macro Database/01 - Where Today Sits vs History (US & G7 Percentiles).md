# Where Today Sits vs History (US & G7 Percentiles)

**What it is** — The own-history percentile framing: instead of asking "is debt high?" in the abstract, we rank each country's *current* reading against its *own* multi-century record. This is the GMD's structural edge in action — the US public-debt series runs back to **1790**, CPI to **1721**, and the UK government accounts to **1700**, so "today vs history" means today vs two-plus centuries, not today vs 1962. See [[00 - GMD Overview & Structural Edge]] for the database build.

## The US today, ranked against its own history (P1)

Three readings, three very different places in the distribution:

| US metric | Today | Own-history percentile | History since |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Public debt / GDP | **122.3%** | **99th** | 1790 (n=235) |
| CPI inflation | **2.9%** | **67th** | 1721 |
| Real long rate (10y − CPI) | **1.3%** | **28th** | 1798 |

The story is the **tension** across those three rows. US debt sits at the **99th percentile** of its entire recorded history — effectively an all-time high outside of wartime peaks. Inflation at the **67th percentile** is moderately elevated but unremarkable by long-run standards (the gold-standard era ran far more deflationary — see the regime note below). Yet the real long rate, at the **28th percentile**, is *historically cheap*: the government is carrying a near-record debt load while paying a real cost of borrowing that is in the bottom third of two centuries.

That combination — **record debt financed at a cheap real rate** — is the single most important fact on this page for a duration view. It is the modern fiscal-dominance configuration: the level looks alarming, but the *carry* of that level is benign, which is exactly why it has been sustainable so far and exactly what unwinds if the term premium re-rates.

## Same level ≠ same extremity: the G7 table (P2)

The percentile lens matters most across countries, because **the same raw debt/GDP number sits in completely different places in each country's own history.** A 100%-debt economy that has never been near 100% before is in a very different spot than one that has twice carried >250% and lived.

| Country | Debt/GDP (2024) | Own-history percentile | History since |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Japan (JPN) | **236.1%** | **96th** | 1872 |
| Italy (ITA) | **134.7%** | **93rd** | 1861 |
| USA | **122.3%** | **99th** | 1790 |
| France (FRA) | **113.2%** | **86th** | 1880 |
| Canada (CAN) | **111.3%** | **94th** | 1867 |
| UK (GBR) | **101.2%** | **54th** | 1700 |
| Germany (DEU) | **62.2%** | **82nd** | 1871 |

### The UK-vs-US contrast — the headline of the whole table

Look at the **UK against the US**. On raw level they are close: GBR **101.2%** vs USA **122.3%**. But on own-history extremity they are worlds apart — the UK sits at only the **54th percentile** of its own record while the US sits at the **99th**.

Why? Because the UK has *been here before, far worse, twice*. Britain twice carried debt above **250%** of GDP — roughly **224%** after Napoleon in 1815, and again after WWII — and serviced its way back down each time without default. So today's 101% is, for the UK, merely the median of its own long history. For the US, 122% is essentially uncharted territory above the WWII peak.

The takeaway for a macro PM: **proven debt capacity is country-specific and is encoded in the percentile, not the level.** A high percentile against a deep history (UK, Japan) signals a sovereign that has demonstrated it can carry and unwind extreme debt; a high percentile that is *also* an all-time high (US) signals a level the institution has never actually tested. Germany at **62.2%** has the lowest raw level in the G7 but still sits at its **82nd** percentile — modest by global standards, high by German standards.

*(Two precedents for "how the unwind happens" — the UK's post-Napoleon grind from 223.8% to 26.9% over 99 years vs the US post-WWII inflation channel from 121.5% to 41.2% over 35 years — are covered in `13 - Macro Cycles & Asset Returns`. The sovereign-default base rate — median default at just **61%** debt/GDP, IQR 32–94% — sits in [[03 - Crisis Aftermath & Recovery Base Rates]] and reinforces that there is no universal "safe" level.)*

### Regime note: why 2.9% inflation is only the 67th percentile

The US inflation distribution is bimodal across regimes. **1800–1970** averaged just **0.85%** with deflation in **37.4%** of years (std 5.99) — frequent deflation was the gold-standard *norm*. **1971–present** averaged **3.81%** with deflation in only **1.7%** of years (std 2.76). So a 2.9% print ranks middling against the full two-century pool precisely because the early record is stuffed with low and negative readings that the fiat era no longer produces. Read the percentile through the regime, not the raw rank.

## What it means for positioning — the duration / term-premium read

- **The asymmetry is in the real rate, not the level.** US debt at the 99th percentile is a slow-moving, well-telegraphed fact; markets have priced it. The live variable is the **real long rate at the 28th percentile**. Record debt is only sustainable while the real cost of financing it stays cheap. The duration trade is therefore fundamentally a *term-premium* trade: if the term premium normalizes off historically depressed levels, the real rate re-rates up the percentile distribution and the "cheap carry on record debt" configuration breaks — bearish duration, steeper curve. This connects directly to the real-yield and curve work in [[Government Bond Yields]] and [[Yield Curve & Recession Signals]].
- **Differentiate sovereigns by percentile, not headline level.** When screening G7 rates and FX, treat the **own-history percentile as the fragility signal**: the US (all-time-high level) is more exposed to a confidence/term-premium shock than the UK (mid-history) or Japan (extreme but with a century-long demonstrated capacity to carry it). Use this to set relative-value tilts across G7 duration and to inform the USD leg in [[USD & G10 FX]].
- **Watch the tension resolve.** The combination of 99th-percentile debt and 28th-percentile real rates is internally unstable over a long horizon: it resolves either through the real rate rising toward debt (bad for bonds) or debt falling toward the cheap rate (fiscal consolidation / inflation, per the two historical roads). Frame US duration risk around *which channel* gets used. Cross-reference the live regime read in [[Macro Regime - Live (June 2026)]].
